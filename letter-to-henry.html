A letter to Henry, following the talk of November 20

Organic and Digital Creativity

Dear Henry,

You got under my skin at the talk on the 20th, in the best way. When I said AI has no original thoughts, you pushed back with two examples I haven't been able to shake off: connecting two distant domains, which we'd call original in any person, and the Go program playing moves no master had ever seen. On the spot I answered that there are two kinds of originality, digital and human. That was a holding position more than an argument. Since then I've been trying to turn it into a real distinction, one I can defend, or that you can knock down properly. Here's where I've got to.

Granting your point first

You're right that a great deal of what we honour as human creativity is combination. Margaret Boden's three kinds of creativity help me here. Combinational creativity joins existing ideas in new ways, which is your cross-domain case. Exploratory creativity searches a space whose rules are already given, which is Go. Transformational creativity changes the rules of the space itself. AI is already superb at the first two, often better than we are. So if there's a line, it has to be drawn at the third, or somewhere else altogether.

I also have to concede that the Go example is stronger than I treated it in the room. AlphaGo's successors learned from no human games at all, only from playing against themselves. So I can't say digital creativity is just a rearrangement of human writing. A better wording is that it finds patterns in representations of a world: text, games, sensor data.

Where I think the line falls: commitment

The strongest objection to my position is that even Einstein was combining. He had Maxwell, Michelson–Morley and Mach, and he simply reached further than anyone else. If transformational creativity is only a very distant combination, my line becomes a matter of degree, and a big enough search might cross it.

My answer comes from Planck. In 1900 he introduced the quantum as what he later called "an act of desperation." He didn't believe in it. He spent years trying to explain it away, and he accepted it only because the blackbody data left him no other way out. What strikes me isn't that he generated the idea. It's that he was stuck with it. He bore the cost of believing it against his own wishes and against the physics he loved.

Thomas Kuhn gives this a larger frame. In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions he separates normal science, the patient puzzle-solving that goes on inside an accepted paradigm, from the revolutions that overturn the paradigm itself. Normal science lines up neatly with Boden's exploratory creativity, and it is exactly where AI shines: AlphaFold is normal science done at superhuman speed. Revolutions begin differently. Anomalies pile up that the paradigm can't absorb. Most practitioners learn to set them aside, and a few can't. Kuhn insists that a paradigm isn't given up on evidence alone. It takes people willing to stake their careers on a new way of seeing before the case is proven. He even cites Planck's own rueful remark that a new scientific truth wins not by converting its opponents but because they eventually die and a new generation grows up with it. That is commitment working at the level of a whole community.

So here's my proposed demarcation. Digital creativity produces candidates. Organic creativity commits to one: it notices that something is wrong when everyone else has learned to live with it, it stakes something on an answer, and it defends that answer at a cost. A model can produce quantization as one option among a thousand. Nothing is at stake for it in which one turns out to be true. The test I keep coming back to is:

Would it argue for this against its own interests?

Why the difference runs deep: the physics

This is where my old trade as a physicist comes in, and I'd love your reading of it. Organic intelligence is the product of evolution. Statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics, driving living matter, constantly probe the world and respond to it. As Schrödinger put it in What Is Life?, a living thing holds itself together far from equilibrium by drawing order from its surroundings. A mind is made of the same physics it is trying to understand. Its thinking is continuous with the world it thinks about.

A chip is the opposite, and by design. Engineers work very hard to shield computation from physics, suppressing noise and fluctuation so that every bit does exactly what it's told. Evolution uses noise. Mutation is variation driven by fluctuation, and our bodies run through countless such events every day, most of them going nowhere and a few going everywhere. Computing removes that noise. So organic intelligence is immersed in nature's randomness, and digital intelligence is sealed off from it. Sensors and robots widen the window somewhat, but they come nowhere near the way we are soaked in the world around us.

Put briefly:

Organic creativity

grows out of living in the world, where things are at stake and consequences have to be borne.

Digital creativity

grows out of searching representations of the world. A Go board is a world with nothing at stake.

The case that will test all this

You'll have seen that on the same day as my talk, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he believes he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis by working with Claude over about six weeks. It's still being checked, and a separate, entirely human proof claim appeared the same day. If it holds up, it will be the strongest evidence yet for your side.

But look at how he put it: working with Claude. A human chose the problem, cared about it for years, judged which avenues were worth pursuing, and put his name and reputation behind the result. To me that looks less like a mathematician being replaced and more like the partnership I described in the talk, where the servant spirit does astonishing work and the commitment stays with the human. I may be wrong, though, and I'd rather find out from you than from the newspapers.

What I'd like to hear from you

These are the places where I think I'm most exposed:

  • Is "commitment" really a feature of the creative act, or only of the social life surrounding it, in which case a machine's idea defended by its human champion would count?
  • The functionalist view, Turing's included, says the substrate doesn't matter, only what a system does. Does my physics argument actually answer that, or does it just restate my intuition in better vocabulary?
  • You know far more than I do about how ideas and texts are interpreted. Is the meaning of an original idea completed only by an interpreting community? If so, is the line between organic and digital creativity drawn not inside the creator but in the reception? Kuhn seems to say yes, at least for science: a paradigm lives in a community, not in a single mind.

I'd much rather continue this over coffee than by email, but put it in writing if that suits you better. And I hope you'll come to the AI Literacy SIG. This is exactly the conversation it was made for.

With warm regards,

John
