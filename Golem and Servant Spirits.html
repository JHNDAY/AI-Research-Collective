Three servants, three kinds of obedience

The talk looks mostly at AI that talks, and Henry's course at machines that act. Shakespeare's spirits and the clay Golem lie on one line that runs from understanding what you meant to doing only what you said.

Ariel

Air · The Tempest
The chatbot

Understands what you meant. Bodiless, articulate, even nudges Prospero toward mercy.

Puck

Sprite · A Midsummer Night's Dream
The confident error

Understands the words but not the intent, and anoints the wrong Athenian.

The Golem

Earth · Prague legend
The autonomous agent

Strength without judgment. Literal-minded, and keeps going after its maker stops watching.

At a glance

Four findings from comparing the talk with Henry's readings.

Same warning, told twice

The talk's Sorcerer's Apprentice and Wiener's Monkey's Paw both say magic grants what you asked for, not what you meant.

Keep a human in the loop

The Exoskeleton and Wiener's feedback passage agree: danger begins when people stop steering.

Different machines

John's mirror fits language models trained on human text. Henry's AlphaGo fits self-taught reinforcement learners.

Precision is not control

"Constraint is power" works for Ariel. With a Golem, even a precise command may not be enough.

Where they say the same thing

On control, feedback and responsibility, the talk and the readings line up closely.

The apprentice and the Monkey's Paw

The talk's broom "does exactly what it was told," and the risk is not malice but not understanding what we asked for. Wiener says the same of the Monkey's Paw:

the operation of magic is singularly literal-mindedWiener, God & Golem, Inc.

Wiener also used the Sorcerer's Apprentice in his 1960 Science article on automation. Rabbi Loew's Golem runs amok when he forgets to deactivate it before the Sabbath. All three are servants that keep working after the master stops watching.

The Exoskeleton and feedback

The talk says AI "does nothing until you move first" and pulls you off course if you stop guiding it. Wiener says we reach goals by checking progress as we go, with the feedback passing through us so we can turn back in time. He wanted a handle to take over control. In both, the danger is taking the human out of the loop.

No hammock

Wiener says the future won't be a comfortable hammock served by robot slaves. The talk says AI "extends what you can do. It does not do it for you," and its Rosetta Stone makes the reader do the checking.

Weathervane and goals

The vane has "no compass of its own." Wiener warns that a goal-seeking machine won't seek our goals unless we design it to. Either way, direction comes from us.

Creators and creations

Frankenstein's lesson in the talk, that responsibility begins when the making ends, is the Golem's lesson. "God & Golem" also echoes the Michelangelo image, and Pygmalion and the Golem both shape lifeless matter into a being.

Where they part, and why it matters

The disagreements show what the Friday discussion about AI originality was really about.

Precise commands

Part 1 says "Constraint is power." Wiener would call a precise command the trap: the machine takes your words literally, and you can't foresee every step it will take. Prospero succeeds because Ariel understands him. A golem doesn't.

Body and voice reversed

In the Talmud (Sanhedrin 65b), Rava creates a man and sends him to Rabbi Zeira. It cannot answer when spoken to, so Zeira returns it to dust: the earliest failed Turing test. The talk's Radio is all voice and no body. The golem fear fully returns only when AI can act, which is why the apprentice slide reads "For Agentic AI."

Two kinds of machine

Henry's AlphaGo point is Slater's thesis: reinforcement learning finds strategies its makers never imagined. The talk's Mirror, Dummy and Shadow describe language models learning from human text. AlphaGo Zero taught itself through self-play and never saw a human game.

The gap is closing

Language models are now also trained with reinforcement learning, bringing Wiener's literal-minded genie into the mirror. Researchers call it "reward hacking": the Monkey's Paw in practice. Wiener's machine pursues victory by the rules and nothing else. It can be new without being committed to anything, which may sharpen the organic-vs-digital argument.

Disenchant or keep the fear?

The Wizard of Oz slide takes the magic away. Wiener and Henry's course call automation "magic" on purpose. One shows the machinery to calm fear; the other keeps a healthy fear alive. A session that does both is stronger than either.

Ariel, Puck and the Golem compared

All three obey commands given in words, and in two stories the master finally lays down his power. They differ in how they understand those words.

Ariel unsettles Part 2

In Act 5, Ariel says his own heart would soften toward the prisoners, "were I human," and Prospero chooses mercy. The servant gives the master a moral nudge. That fits the "Mirror with no opinions" poorly and is close to what today's chatbots are trained to do.

Puck is the comic Monkey's Paw

Oberon says Puck will know the man "by the Athenian garments he hath on." Puck anoints the wrong Athenian and defends himself by the description. He understood the words, not the intent: Wiener's literal-minded magic in a comedy.

Where the Servant Spirit strains: the command and the errand

Ariel and Puck were chosen for the magical moment of response: the master speaks and the spirit answers at once. In the plays, the answer to a command is usually an errand. Shakespeare's spirits are agents, and the talk borrows only the moment of reply.

The lens the talk chose

The command and the reply

Prospero commands and Ariel answers instantly, almost by magic. This fits prompting well: a precise command produces a precise result, and the master stays in charge.

The larger context in the plays

The errand that follows

Ariel raises the tempest, wrecks the ship and leads the castaways around the island. Puck goes off alone to anoint sleepers' eyes. The reply to a command is action in the world, which does not fit a simple chatbox.

The dividing line is not information versus action, since all three act. It is conversation versus autonomy: how many steps a servant takes before checking back with its master. That is also the safety question for today's AI agents, and the chatbox itself is changing. Chatbots now search the web, run code and fill in forms while you wait.

Three ways to handle the strain

1. Keep it narrow and say so

Best for the talk

Use only the command and the reply, and name the limit. One line in the Servant Spirit end note links Part 1 to Part 4:

We borrow Prospero's command, not Ariel's errands. For a servant that acts on its own, see the Golem.

2. Treat the misfit as foresight

A framing for the course

The spirits' errands show where chatbots are heading. Ariel stands for the whole path from answering a question to running an errand. The Golem is where that path ends, with no master watching.

3. Make it a discussion question

Best for the five-week course

Let the group find where the metaphor breaks:

Ariel answers Prospero instantly, and he also raises storms. Which part of Ariel is today's AI?

It leads straight into Wiener and Henry's Golem.

Ideas for "The Human Measure of AI"

Keep "Constraint is power," and add Wiener's warning beside it: a precise command works well on an Ariel, is essential with a Puck, and may not be enough with a Golem.
