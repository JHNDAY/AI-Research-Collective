By Steven Rattner · Read the original in The New York Times

The walls are closing in on A.I.: data center moratoriums, job fears, safety alarms. The author calls the worries valid, yet says we need A.I. to deliver.

Workers rarely get more productive on their own. Investment and training help, but the essay names technological innovation as the main driver. Higher national income also gives the country room to reduce inequality, hold down debt and fund health care and education.

Productivity moves with invention

The essay traces how living standards stood still for millennia, surged with each wave of innovation, then stalled in the early 1970s.

  1. Before 1500
    Little innovation, little change in living standards.
  2. c. 1500
    Productivity starts to grow as farming modernizes, modestly.
  3. Mid-1800s
    Industrial Revolution: mechanized looms and steam power.
  4. Late 1800s to 1900s
    Telegraph, telephone, railroads, combustion engine, electricity: a golden age.
  5. Early 1970s
    Productivity growth slows and stays depressed for about 50 years.
  6. Late 1990s
    The internet boom lifts productivity sharply, after Solow's 1987 quip.

Jobs: some vanish, others appear

The author's historical claim is that no technological improvement has ultimately destroyed more jobs than it created. He points to his own start on Wall Street in 1982, when spreadsheets were typed by a secretarial pool; the menial work disappeared, yet finance employs far more people today.

Boosted so far

Losses to come

  • A.I. has so far killed a limited number of jobs
  • More losses are probable, with offsetting gains elsewhere
  • Telephone operators are the historical parallel

Who has gained, and who should pay

So far the gains have gone to investors in A.I. companies, not to workers. The essay says the cost of helping displaced workers should fall on A.I.'s beneficiaries.

Help for workers

  • Retraining and education subsidies for younger workers
  • A stronger safety net for older workers, possibly wage insurance for those displaced
  • Securing the solvency of Social Security and Medicare

Who pays

  • Consider a “token tax” on A.I. usage
  • Curb the generous tax benefits for A.I. capital spending
  • End state sales and property tax exemptions for data centers
  • Edge the corporate tax rate back toward 35 percent

Safety: manageable, not eliminated

The author compares A.I. to other powerful technologies that nations have regulated safely, including food, medicine, aviation and nuclear energy. He recalls growing up with air raid drills and films like Fail Safe.

What he proposes

  • Government testing of new A.I. products before deployment
  • Consider mandating kill switches against models that go rogue
  • International cooperation, especially with China

Why it is harder than nuclear control

  • A.I. technology crosses borders more easily
  • But powerful models need chips and data centers, so the U.S. lead in the build-out is an asset for keeping A.I. under control
