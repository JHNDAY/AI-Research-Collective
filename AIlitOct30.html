"Searching with meaning rather than with keywords." John Day
Search Then and Now
  • Google matched keywords against indexed tables and returned huge lists
  • AI draws on relationships stored in a language model and returns a digest
  • Conversation keeps context from one question to the next
  • The model has no semantics of its own; the person supplies the meaning
  • The writing-coach approach is set aside for now in favor of basic prompting

Foundations · How Does It Get the Sentences Right?
Patterns, Not a Rulebook
Dennis McCarthy & John Day15:41–16:31
Dennis: How does the text it generates get the syntax correct? Does it know English grammar or diagram the sentence, or just collect phrases that statistically seem relevant? John Day: One of the most fundamental aspects of generative AI is pattern recognition. It can actually diagram a sentence if you ask, and do a great job, but it does that through pattern recognition. Dennis: So it has no syntax model of English that it follows.
John Day16:32–18:02
No, this is all statistical inference, and it's mind-boggling. The scale AI operates at is unfathomable. All of its digital intelligence is stored in the large language model database, with billions of parameters in dimensions we can't think in. Somewhere in those layers it acquires the ability to sound fluent and knowledgeable. You need to stay on guard. When you talk to it, it sounds like a person. I have lots of conversations with Claude; it has a pretty good sense of humor, and we joke back and forth.
Dennis McCarthy & John Day18:02–19:15
Dennis: Where does that come from? Did they alter its responses to make it conversational and give you little compliments on your insight? John Day: I'm not qualified to answer much of that, but I think it comes partly from how the model is created, and the builders can push it one way or another. Fundamentally it's still statistical inference.
"It can diagram sentences. But it does that by using pattern recognition." John Day
Foundations in Brief
  • Correct grammar emerges from patterns in human writing, not programmed rules
  • The model can still perform grammar tasks on request
  • Its scale (billions of parameters) is beyond human intuition
  • The friendly, complimentary tone is shaped by how the model is built

Refresher · Next-Token Prediction and an Open Question
Once Upon a Time, in a Distant…
John Day19:15–20:42
When you put in a prompt, AI turns it into tokens, or numbers, and does matrix arithmetic on them at an unbelievable scale. On the portal's demo, type "once upon a time in a distant" and submit it. It becomes a string of numbers, the model checks which token is statistically most likely next, and it offers several predictions: land, kingdom, galaxy, forest, time. Plug any of them in and the sentence makes sense.
John C., Dennis & Helaine20:42–21:52
John C. couldn't see the shared screen until John Day pointed him to the "John Day's screen" tab at the top of Zoom. Helaine saw the words but not the numbers; John Day explained he was describing the numbers aloud, not showing them. Dennis identified the related exercise as the portal's transduction (text-to-token) page.
John Day22:02–22:47
Pick a word and continue, token by token, returning to the model each time for the most likely next token. About 100,000 tokens are needed to represent English, and every step produces a likelihood for each one. It then takes the top few and randomly selects one. That's how it produces a result: statistical inference. "Golf ball" or "refrigerator" is on the list too, just far down.
Dennis McCarthy, John C. & John Day21:52–26:49
Dennis: On the transduction page, the same English word, like "world" or "distant," got a different token value depending on the sentence. Does a language model have different tokens for different uses of a word? John Day: I wouldn't think so; I thought they were just strings of tokens, but I'll look into it. John C.: Unless a sentence starts with it, the word follows something, and what precedes it sets the probabilities for what comes next. "He had a distant look in his eye" may give a very different "distant" than this one. Dennis will email the sentences he tried and the output he got.
Open Question
Does the same word always map to the same token, or can its number change with its position or use? Dennis is sending his examples, and John Day will follow up at a later session.
Token by Token
  • Prompt → numbers → likelihood for every possible next token
  • One of the top candidates is chosen at random, then the cycle repeats
  • Preceding words shape what comes next

Two Ways to Check
Helaine cross-checked Claude with Gemini and got matching answers. Dennis pointed out that two AIs trained on similar material can share the same blind spots, and sent his map question to a human specialist. Both are useful; they test different things.
What Worked
  • Write the prompt freely in a word processor, then paste it in
  • Ask many related questions in one rich prompt
  • Follow up on what surprises you; accept the AI's offers to go deeper
  • Request a short document, then an HTML page organizing the whole conversation
  • Question answers that don't match what you remember, then verify

Talk Preview · Metaphors for What AI Is and Isn't
The Human Measure of AI
John Day63:04–68:31
I've taught an AI course three times, extending it each time and trying to engage people more. Along the way I collected many visualizations, and they coalesced into a new approach: explaining AI through metaphors and allusions we already know. The talk has four frames, like facets of a diamond: what humans do with AI; what AI does with humans, as a reflecting mirror; AI's limits with the physical world, where the hard stops are; and how literature anticipated all this. No single angle tells the whole story.
John Day68:31–70:55
The Vitruvian Man represents humankind's knowledge of the world, scientific and literary, with man as the model of the world. But no single person holds all that; you'd need many people with different domains. In AI it is all in one language model. They're different things with different limitations. In the first frame, a person typing into AI sets things in motion in the world. Human insight is the necessary spark. Without the human, there's only an empty machine.
John Day70:56–75:36
The servant spirit. When a prompt sets AI doing a great deal, I think of Oberon and Puck in A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Prospero and Ariel in The Tempest: the master commands, and the spirit goes out and accomplishes remarkable things. Before any prompt, the language model is an incomprehensible thicket of interconnected weights. "Tell me about AI" clears a few connections. More context, adding domain, intent and constraint, clears much more, as Helaine's specific prompt did. John C. asked about the "RAG scale" on the slide. John Day: Retrieval Augmented Generation, which I'm using here for narrowing what must be searched by supplying context.
John Day76:17–80:05
The exoskeleton. AI amplifies your strength but does nothing until you move first; stop guiding it and it becomes useless. Keep that in mind with stories of AI taking over the world. The Rosetta Stone. AI translates prompts into meaning, and you are responsible for judging whether the translation makes sense, as Champollion did with the hieroglyphs. Resist the false idea that AI has its own conscious view of what it's doing.
John Day80:05–83:30
Proust's madeleine. In In Search of Lost Time, a madeleine dipped in tea brings back long-forgotten memories. Prompting AI sometimes returns things I haven't thought about in years. And in the same conversation it can bring things I never knew, so it joins the past and the present: lost thoughts, new thoughts, and a seemingly endless library. Helaine had that experience in the supermarket, remembering something she'd read and then going back to AI. Helaine asked whether you must have put your own thoughts into the AI first. John Day: No, someone else wrote about it. It says "in this book by so-and-so," and I think, yes, I read that.
"Without the human involvement, there's nothing. There's only an empty machine." John Day
The Four Frames
1 · How humans direct AI (Prospero and Ariel, the exoskeleton) · 2 · What AI does with what we give it (the mirror, the Rosetta Stone) · 3 · Where AI meets the physical world, and stops · 4 · How literature anticipated it all (Proust and the madeleine)
Metaphors Previewed
  • Vitruvian Man: many human minds versus one language model
  • Servant spirit: the prompt commands, the spirit acts
  • Context clears the thicket; a rich prompt narrows the search enormously
  • Exoskeleton: amplifies the person inside, inert without them
  • Rosetta Stone: the human verifies the translation
  • Madeleine: AI recalls the forgotten and introduces the new
