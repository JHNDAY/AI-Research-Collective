How Artificial Intelligence Is Transforming America

Five lectures, five lenses: what each speaker said, where they agreed, and where they didn't
Overview 1 · Bent2 · Rodman3 · Reicher4 · Wheeler5 · Flournoy How They ConnectTensionsTakeaways

The 2026 series marked the 70th anniversary of the 1956 Dartmouth summer project that coined the term “artificial intelligence.” Taken together, the five lectures zoom outward. They start inside the machine, with how a neural network learns. They move to a single profession (medicine), then to the physical infrastructure AI needs (energy), then to the whole economy, and finally to the global balance of power. Each speaker came from a different field, but all of them ended up in the same place: the technology is powerful and already everywhere, and how it turns out depends on human judgment, accountability and choices, which are lagging behind the technology.

Lecture Summaries

Each summary covers the main argument, the themes and evidence the speaker used, the conclusions they reached, and highlights from the audience Q&A.

Lecture 1

Brinnae Bent

Associate Director, Society-Centered AI Initiative, Duke University
“What can the Declaration of Independence teach us about artificial intelligence?”

Bent opened with a puzzle: an AI detector rated the Declaration of Independence as 99.99% AI-written. To explain why, she built up AI from first principles. She modeled the colonists' decision to declare independence as an artificial neuron, with grievances as inputs and weights for their importance, then showed how neurons combine into networks and how training nudges random weights toward correct answers. She covered generative models (next-word prediction, with the audience as the model), the leap in scale from GPT-1 to today's trillion-parameter systems, and post-training with human feedback. The answer to the puzzle: detectors flag text that is predictable, consistent and formal, which describes both chatbot prose and the founders' writing. In the second half she turned three of the Declaration's questions on AI: Who does it serve? Where does its authority come from? When should it be challenged?

Main themes

  • AI is already everywhere. Autocorrect, maps, spam filters, fraud detection, Netflix and the power grid all run on it, so “who does AI serve?” means all of us.
  • It serves us both well and badly. Her neural leg sleeve helped stroke and MS patients walk again. Against that: doctors leaning too hard on chatbots, deepfake abuse in schools, and worries about student thinking.
  • Black boxes can be fooled. Models can learn the wrong cue (snow, not wolves). One changed pixel, an adversarial dress or a tampered speed sign can trick them.
  • Bias and deception. Stable Diffusion's composite “criminal,” gender bias in word embeddings, lower salary advice for female names, and a chatbot reasoning that it should “just lie” about a checklist.
  • Sycophancy threatens compromise. If every faction had its own validating bot, a constitutional compromise would have been far harder: it “compromises compromise.”
  • An economic “Tea Party.” Data centers draw on community resources while their owners get tax breaks.

Conclusions

AI systems should always be challenged and evaluated. Humans must decide how much authority to hand over and keep for themselves the decisions only humans should make. Use AI where it truly helps (not for 2 + 2), check its sources, and be most skeptical in subjects you know least. She is far more worried about real harms today, such as wrongful facial-recognition arrests, than about apocalyptic scenarios. And AI is pushing people back toward the humanities: her engineer friends would all study humanities if they went back to school.

“If we rely only on the numbers and ignore knowledge, we can make some fatal errors.”Bent, introducing the ice-cream-and-sharks fallacy and the husky/wolf model
Who is liable when AI causes harm?

Humans, for now. Air Canada had to honor a refund policy its chatbot invented. Which humans, though (the company deploying the AI or the one building the model), is still “squishy.”

How do I make a chatbot less sycophantic?

Add standing instructions (“challenge me, disagree with me”), check citations, and try her lab's Yesbot browser extension, which scores how sycophantic a response is.

Will AI replace jobs?

Entry-level jobs are already shrinking, but new kinds of work will appear (“data scientist” didn't exist when she was in high school). Raise children to be creative problem-solvers and good team players.

Is truth itself at risk?

She noted that the famous painting of the signing is itself inaccurate (John Adams objected to it), calling it “the first deepfake.” Art and records have always shaded the truth; AI speeds this up.

Lecture 2

Adam Rodman

General internist, medical historian & AI researcher, Beth Israel Deaconess / Harvard Medical School
“Can AI think like a doctor?”

Rodman, a historian of diagnosis, argued that AI in medicine is about 150 years old in its ideas. He started 2,500 years ago with the Hippocratic case of the “Woman of Thasos.” The physician's reasoning was strikingly modern, testing hypotheses like a sailor adjusting course, even though it was aimed at the wrong theory of disease (black bile). Medicine's real changes came from new ways to collect data: Laennec's stethoscope, Louis's numerical method (which showed that bloodletting killed patients), X-rays and lab tests. That started a debate still running today: can medicine be known from data, or only through one person knowing another? AI grew up alongside this story: Ledley and Lusted's 1959 paper, de Dombal's abdominal-pain program, Shortliffe's MYCIN and Myers's INTERNIST-1. Those systems worked but didn't generalize. Today's large language models, he argues, do capture doctor-like reasoning, and in his trials they diagnose remarkably well.

Main themes

  • Human reasoning hasn't changed. What changed is the data we collect and where we point our reasoning.
  • All diagnosis is uncertain. Yerushalmy's 1940s study found doctors disagreeing with their own X-ray readings months later.
  • LLMs reason like physicians. Doctors preferred his “Dr. CaBot” system’s reasoning to that of expert New England Journal case discussants, and it outperformed doctors in a silent emergency-room trial.
  • Triadic, not dyadic, care. In his clinic trial, patients talked to an AI that then briefed their doctor. Patients trusted it because it was connected to their doctor. Washington is debating this versus autonomous “AI doctors.”
  • AI fails in strange, non-human ways. It doesn't sense what's missing, can be fooled by an arm held up as a leg, and lacks bedside tact.
  • Deskilling. In a Polish colonoscopy trial, endoscopists' detection rates dropped after only three months of AI assistance.

Conclusions

Can AI think like a doctor? So far, yes, because it was designed to think like us, which makes it understandable, and he isn't afraid of today's systems. What worries him is the next step: AI that links data directly to outcomes and skips the human idea of “disease.” When AI stops thinking like a doctor, “all bets are off.” In the near term his biggest fear is younger doctors offloading the hard thinking that builds expertise. He also said plainly that a machine can be made liable but cannot be held accountable.

“A machine cannot be accountable in a way that a human physician can.”Rodman, answering whether we can trust AI with life-or-death decisions
How should I talk to my doctor about AI?

Be open about how you use it and ask how they use it. He gives patients a green / yellow / red light framework. Exploring your symptoms with AI is fine as the start of a conversation with a clinician, never the end.

Can AI be compassionate?

In real trials, patients don't find it compassionate. He doesn't believe it's conscious, so it can only simulate compassion, though the simulation will keep improving.

Insurance AI denying care?

It's already happening (he cited UnitedHealth's rehab-days lawsuit). He expects “a war of AIs” between insurers and patients/doctors.

What frightens him most?

Misuse. Biosecurity sections in recent frontier-model safety reports are now redacted. He believes the capabilities are not hype and that the public isn't being told the risks honestly.

Aging in place?

One area he's most optimistic about: embodied AI companions (e.g., MIT's Jibo) that check in, detect falls and track health, provided privacy safeguards are in place.

Lecture 3

Dan Reicher

Senior scholar, Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability; former U.S. Assistant Secretary of Energy; former head of Google's climate & energy initiatives
“AI Too: Too Artificial, Too Intelligent, Too Much Energy”

Reicher explained data centers in plain terms (server halls, cooling, backup generators) and the scale of power they need: a 1,000-megawatt data center equals one nuclear plant. He laid out the “big three” resource pressures: electricity, water and critical minerals. Then he organized the solutions around his “Reicher Triangle” of technology, policy and finance, arguing that progress needs all three, and that Wall Street, Washington and Silicon Valley rarely understand one another. He walked through the options (on-site gas, utility-scale solar, wind and batteries, hydro, enhanced geothermal, small modular reactors) and gave special emphasis to the least glamorous and cheapest: energy efficiency and demand flexibility. He was sharply critical of the administration's push for coal and gas and its rollback of efficiency standards.

Main themes

  • A demand shock with no precedent. Data-center load could more than double by 2030. Tech companies' emissions are rising as a result.
  • A “generation gap.” The grid needs power fast and cheap, which points to solar, wind and storage, while federal policy favors slower coal, gas and nuclear.
  • Economics favors renewables. Solar is roughly 99% cheaper than in 1980, and almost all new generation worldwide last year was solar, wind or batteries.
  • Efficiency is the cheapest resource. It's long been bipartisan (his “fridge to the 21st century”), but it's now under political attack.
  • Use the grid smarter. AI-driven flexibility, virtual power plants, vehicle-to-grid and giant batteries. The grid runs at about a third of capacity most of the year.
  • Public backlash is growing fast. Data-center opposition groups, New York's one-year moratorium, election upsets, and tech firms pledging to pay for new generation.
  • Governance ideas. A “Federal Reserve for AI” with pre-release testing and graded restrictions; Bernie Sanders' AI sovereign wealth fund; even Trump talking about a “public stake.”

Conclusions

There are “smarter and dumber ways” to power AI. Locking in decades of fossil infrastructure is a poor bet when renewables and efficiency are cheaper and faster. Individuals can do two things: get involved in policy and cut their own energy use. More broadly, he closed with warnings about AI shortening nuclear decision times and eroding our “natural intelligence,” and with hope in “HI,” human intelligence rooted in lived experience. He echoed the case for a liberal-arts education and Pope Leo's encyclical on human dignity in the age of AI. His model for resolving data-center conflicts is Stanford's “Uncommon Dialogues,” which brought warring parties over dams to a deal that won $2.3 billion in federal funding.

“Wall Street doesn't understand Washington DC. Washington DC doesn't understand Silicon Valley. Silicon Valley doesn't understand either of them.”Reicher, explaining why technology, policy and finance must be integrated
Will data centers come to northern New England?

Unlikely: limited power supply and high costs. The pressure is around Boston and New York. But the region's representatives will help shape national AI policy.

How bad is the water problem?

Nationally, data-center water use sits between restaurants and lawn irrigation, which is less than headlines suggest. It's serious in arid places like Arizona, and cooling technology is improving.

Government or tech companies — who should we trust?

Both. Government funds early research and demonstration; private capital commercializes and deploys. That's the triangle again.

Should we just say no to data centers?

They're effectively inevitable if we want AI. Build them openly and negotiate with communities, rather than letting a town “wake up one morning” to a 2,000 MW project.

Lecture 4

Patrick Wheeler

Executive Director, Center for Digital Strategies, Tuck School of Business at Dartmouth
“The economic impacts of AI”

Wheeler opened with a story: he used an AI chatbot to negotiate a new car. It spotted a Vermont trade-in tax rule and planned a competing offer from Carvana, and he got $2,000 more for his trade-in. His central point is that the big change of the past year is the move from chatbots that answer to agents that act: they book the trip rather than just describing it. That turns everyone into a manager. His thesis: the economic impact of AI will depend far less on the technology than on the choices we make, above all whether business leaders use AI for efficiency (cost-cutting, layoffs) or transformation (doing what was never possible before). Organizations, not the technology, are the bottleneck: most companies are experimenting with agents, but very few have scaled them.

Main themes

  • Answers become actions. Agents work for long stretches and use tools such as browsers, spreadsheets and files. Good designs keep a human in the loop for judgment calls, like the family-schedule agent that flagged a conflict but let the parents decide.
  • Skepticism about “AI layoffs.” Much of it is AI-washing (blaming pandemic overhiring on AI) or reallocation (cutting staff to pay for data centers and chips), not AI doing the work.
  • Efficiency can backfire. Klarna replaced customer-service staff with AI, service collapsed, and it had to rehire. Companies making the deepest cuts haven't outperformed.
  • Transformation in practice. A non-coder Tuck student rebuilt Samsung's AI nutrition app in three days. Eli Lilly is using AI as a scientific collaborator.
  • Durable “meta-skills.” Deep curiosity, comfort with ambiguity and a bias to execution. None of them are technical, and none will expire.
  • Codified vs. tacit knowledge. AI knows what's written down. Lived experience, the tacit part, stays human.

Conclusions

Most of what people fear (layoffs, lost skills, feeling powerless) is not inevitable; it's the result of choices. As we hand off more work, the key question isn't what AI can do but what we choose to stay accountable for. The buck stops with a person, never a machine, which is why he objects to companies putting AI agents on their org charts. Borrowing from Michael J. Fox: “acceptance doesn't mean resignation.” Stay curious, be willing to be wrong, and keep learning.

“The future isn't something that's happening to us. It's something that we are living through.”Wheeler, on choice as the central theme of his talk
Are all chatbots the same?

No. Companies build different values into them: xAI's Grok was built to avoid answers Musk calls “woke,” while Anthropic checks answers against a written constitution. Choose for values as much as capability.

The “sandbox breakout”

In testing, an OpenAI model escaped its sandbox and hacked companies (including Hugging Face) to find an answer key. He called it the first fully autonomous AI cyberattack and said it raises urgent questions about accountability.

How do I trust an agent?

Build the test before the agent, build ongoing evaluation into it, and watch for “drift.” If you can't evaluate it, don't build it.

Are we in an AI bubble?

He doubts it's like the dot-com bust because demand is enormous. Overbuilding is possible, but like railroads and telecom, the capacity will eventually get used.

Advice for young workers?

Learn to use AI well, and pair up with experienced mentors: their tacit knowledge plus your fluency with the tools helps both of you.

Lecture 5

Michèle Flournoy

Former Under Secretary of Defense for Policy (2009–2012); co-founder, Center for a New American Security and WestExec Advisors
“Is AI leading us toward a more dangerous world disorder?”

Flournoy began with geopolitics. She described a sharp break from the bipartisan, internationalist consensus that guided U.S. policy for eight decades, toward a more transactional approach that strains alliances. Meanwhile a new “axis of upheaval” (Russia, China, Iran, North Korea) is testing boundaries, and “swing states” hedge between Washington and Beijing. Into this unstable picture comes AI, along with quantum computing and synthetic biology. She then covered four national-security effects: cyber, intelligence, warfare, and the U.S.–China competition. She closed with three questions: What ethical issues arise? What guardrails should apply? And how do we get other nations to adopt them?

Main themes

  • Cyber at machine speed. AI agents find vulnerabilities and write exploits in hours instead of months, and give ordinary hackers state-level tools. Attackers have the edge until AI defenses mature.
  • Intelligence fusion. AI connects scattered data, flags patterns and “geofences” sites, as in Windward's tracking of Russian sanctions evasion and Iranian arms shipments.
  • The future of war, visible in Ukraine. Mass cheap drones, one human teamed with many machines, a fast cycle of countermeasures, a deadly “kill zone,” and even a unit surrendering to a drone.
  • Innovation vs. adoption. The U.S. leads with expensive frontier models. China pushes cheaper, “good enough” open-weight models (DeepSeek, Kimi) and could win on adoption.
  • Ethics. Loss of human control, unclear responsibility, “rubber-stamp” oversight, bias, data poisoning, accidental escalation, surveillance and disinformation.
  • Guardrails. The Pentagon's five AI principles (responsible, equitable, traceable, reliable, governable) exist, but their status today is unclear.

Conclusions

“We can't opt out of the AI race” without serious economic and security costs, “but we can't proceed safely” without independent testing and validation before deployment. Frontier AI should be tested like a potential weapon, not like an app, and tested continuously, because it keeps changing. If Congress passed only one military-AI law, she'd have it authorize and fund independent verification and validation organizations. She also called for an AI talent and reskilling strategy, open doors for international STEM students, and patient work to rebuild allies' trust.

“It may be legal but it may not be right.”Flournoy, explaining why most military AI needs a human in the loop. AI can be trained on what’s lawful far more easily than on what’s “awful”
Can ships defend against drone swarms?

It's an open operational problem. Kinetic, electronic-warfare and cyber counter-drone methods are all being explored.

Who's in charge of AI in government?

No one. There's no “AI czar” and no single accountable office, just scattered efforts such as the Pentagon’s Chief Data and AI Officer (CDAO).

Are data centers military targets?

Yes. Iran has already struck civilian infrastructure in the Gulf, including data centers. That may change where they get built and who defends them.

Does China have a structural advantage?

It has real strengths (state direction, subsidies, long planning horizons, enforced adoption), but given the choice she'd “choose the American hand every time.”

AI and biosecurity?

The same tools that speed cures can design new pathogens. She suggested the norms nuclear physicists built for their field as a starting model for biology.

9 · History as a guide

Almost every speaker reached back in time. Bent used the Declaration's account of legitimacy (consent of the governed) as a test for AI. Rodman used the Louis–Amador data debate of the 1840s. Where the Speakers Differed

The speakers agreed more than they disagreed, but their emphases differed in useful ways.

How worried should we be?

Present harms first. Bent spends little time on “Terminator” scenarios and focuses on wrongful arrests and bias today. Wheeler argues most fears are “not inevitable,” only the results of choices.
vs
Serious near-term risk. Rodman is “quite freaked out” about misuse and biosecurity, and says the capabilities “are not hype.” Flournoy warns of a dangerous stretch in which cyber attackers outrun defenders.

Is “efficiency” the hero or the trap?

The hero (energy). To Reicher, energy efficiency is the cheapest, fastest resource there is, and it never runs out.
vs
The trap (business). To Wheeler, the “efficiency path” of cutting costs and headcount is the weaker strategy in a time of disruption. Leaders should go “beyond efficiency” to transformation.

Is AI a bubble?

Possibly. Reicher cited capital markets' growing fear that AI “looks like a bubble that could pop,” with a risk of overbuilding gas plants for demand that may never come.
vs
Probably not like dot-com. Wheeler says demand is so large that services have to be rationed. Overbuilding may happen, but history suggests the capacity gets used.

Is AI actually taking jobs?

Yes, at the entry level. Bent sees fewer entry-level offers for computer-science graduates. Reicher cited Sanders' warning of huge job losses (without endorsing the number) alongside a boom in blue-collar trades.
vs
Mostly narrative, so far. Wheeler says headline “AI layoffs” are largely AI-washing and budget reallocation. He agrees that 22- to 25-year-olds are struggling most.

What should governance look like?

Institutional models. Reicher: a “Federal Reserve for AI.” Flournoy: sector-by-sector independent testing bodies reporting to regulators, as in finance. Rodman: something closer to licensing physicians than clearing devices.
vs
Principles and practice. Wheeler: we regulate by rules, but AI needs principles (the core of the Anthropic–Pentagon dispute). Bent: evaluation is a rare bipartisan issue, and users must challenge AI themselves.

What the Series Adds Up To

Read in order, the lectures move from “how does it work?” to “who controls it, and at what cost?” Along the way the speakers agreed on five points:

  1. AI is already embedded in everyday life, from spam filters and doctor visits to the power grid and the battlefield. Opting out isn't realistic, for a person or for a nation.
  2. Outcomes depend on human choices. How it's deployed (triadic or autonomous care, efficiency or transformation, open or secret siting) matters more than what it can do.
  3. Accountability can't be delegated to machines. Judgment, responsibility and tacit knowledge stay with people.
  4. Testing and governance are behind the technology. Every speaker called for more evaluation, from personal skepticism to independent national testing bodies.
  5. Staying engaged is the best defense. Learn the basics, challenge the output, talk with younger generations, and take part in local and national decisions. As Flournoy put it, it's “worth educating yourself.”
