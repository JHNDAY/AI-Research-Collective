What Would Keynes Say About A.I.? The Keynes Bot Told Us.

A Princeton economist rebuilt John Maynard Keynes as an A.I. avatar and asked him whether technology would finally free us from work.

New York Times Opinion · Sept. 30, 2026 · Markus Brunnermeier (Princeton), video by Sarah Kramer and Mehron Abdi

Read the original article and watch the video at The New York Times

The premise

In 1930, Keynes predicted that technology would raise productivity so sharply that people in wealthy countries would work far less within a century. Skeptics doubted him then. Today's A.I. boosters and critics are having much the same argument.

Markus Brunnermeier and his team built a bot trained on more than 12,000 pages of Keynes's writings and transcripts, plus the few recordings of him that exist. Then Brunnermeier interviewed it. Times Opinion distilled the long conversation into a short video exchange.

The conversation, step by step

The exchange moves from vindication to warning to a call for action. The arc is a real sequence, so it is numbered.

  1. Prediction partly confirmed

    Brunnermeier recalls that the essay appeared at the start of the Great Depression. The bot notes America's wealth target was beaten, but the leisure it expected has not followed.

  2. A.I. as a new complication

    Asked whether A.I. could finally deliver the 15-hour week, the bot says it fears a split: overworked professionals with money but no time, and everyone else with time but no money.

  3. Expertise concentrates

    As A.I. absorbs more sophisticated tasks, the bot argues, expertise gathers in a few hands, and those few might end up managing much of the economy.

  4. The "nervous breakdown" returns

    Brunnermeier raises Keynes's warning that humanity, bred to fight scarcity, may struggle once the fight is over. The bot says the strain would hit two groups in different ways (see below).

  5. Not inevitable

    The bot insists the outcome is not fixed by the technology. It depends on whether government has the political courage to use laws and taxes so that A.I. benefits all citizens.

  6. The question handed back

    The bot closes by returning to Keynes's old question about what to do with growing powers of production and invention, and says the answer is up to us.

How the author represented Keynes as an A.I. entity

The Keynes in this video is not a single thing. It is a stack of choices, some made by a language model and many made by people. The article is unusually open about several of them, and silent about others.

The five layers of the avatar

Open each layer. The stack runs from the historical source at the top to the finished video at the bottom.

Source: Keynes's own words

The agent was trained on more than 12,000 pages of Keynes's writings and transcripts. The team instructed it to draw mainly from "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren," the 1930 essay at the heart of the conversation, while keeping the wider body of work available.

Practical effect: the bot is anchored to one prediction, so its answers orbit that essay.

Generation: ChatGPT in a prescribed voice

Brunnermeier and Ph.D. student Pablo Balsinde built the bot using ChatGPT. They told it to answer in the first person, with early 20th-century British cadence and restrained wit.

The article notes the language itself was model-generated, which means the bot's sentences are plausible in Keynes's manner but are not things he wrote.

Human editing: rephrasing, quotes, cuts

First drafts were often long-winded and unclear. The team asked the model to rephrase, prompted it to answer with famous Keynes quotations, and cut parts of responses. The research team also edited responses for clarity and brevity.

Practical effect: what viewers hear is a curated edit of many possible answers.

Translation: modern English, shortened

The narrator admits that Keynes wrote and spoke more formally than we do, so the language was updated to contemporary English and the conversation was trimmed for brevity. What aired is an edited transcript of an interview that was longer and in-depth.

Embodiment: face, desk and voice

Face: the team uploaded a public-domain image of Keynes and used DeepSeek to change the surroundings, placing him behind a desk.

Voice: audio resembling Keynes was created using a BBC recording of him as a reference.

Greeting: the bot opens by noting how unexpected it is to be reanimated as an A.I. so long after 1883, a self-aware line that tells viewers it knows it is a revival.

The deeper the layer, the more it is a person's or a model's construction rather than Keynes's record.

How the video frames the bot for viewers

Signals of authenticity

The bot speaks as "I," in period cadence, and answers Brunnermeier by name. It addresses viewers as the "grandchildren" Keynes wrote about.

Keynes's descendants were consulted: his family members gave the go-ahead.

Archival Keynes audio appears in the video itself, which invites comparison with the synthetic voice.

Signals of artifice

The narrator interrupts repeatedly, admits the language was modernized, and says plainly that they cannot know whether Keynes would approve.

The article describes the construction methods openly, including edits for clarity.

A disclosure notes the Times has sued OpenAI and Microsoft, a relevant fact since ChatGPT is the engine behind the bot.

Who is making the argument?

The bot and the narrator do not say quite the same thing. Compare their conclusions.

The Keynes bot

The outcome is not preordained. It will depend on whether government has the political courage to use laws and taxes so A.I. benefits everyone. The choice is yours.

The narrator

Says it is "obvious" that the power of A.I. cannot end up with the few, and that ongoing regulation and government intervention are needed.

The bot frames a choice and names levers (laws and tax systems). The narrator turns that into a firmer policy conclusion. That shift is worth noticing: the A.I. persona hands the editorial argument a borrowed authority.

Questions for discussion

  • When a model speaks in a historical figure's voice, whose argument are we hearing: the figure's, the model's, or the editors'?
  • Does the openness about methods (editing, modernizing, cutting) make the bot more trustworthy or less?
  • Would the bot's warning land differently from an ordinary expert commentator? Why?
  • Which of Keynes's two predictions, abundance or leisure, do you think A.I. is more likely to affect?
