Lead Story · The Basics of AI Explained in Metaphors

Metaphors Meet Their First Audience — and Push Back

John Day previews his talk on what AI is and how it works, built on four quadrants and a gallery of AI-made images from Leonardo to Disney. The audience answers with questions on originality, emotion, minority views and the off switch.

Presenter: John Day  ·  Attendees: Henry · Jenny Fariborz · Kenneth Jacobs · Klavdia · John & Cheryl (shared connection)

John Day opened by telling the group they were his first audience for this talk and asking for their help. He would explain AI through metaphors and everyday images instead of technical terms and diagrams, and he wanted to hear both which images clicked and which fell flat. The talk is a preview of a course he has already submitted for next spring. That course will go deeper into each concept, look at live online examples and, most importantly, put the tools in participants' hands, because “the best way to understand AI is to use it.”

The framework rests on Leonardo da Vinci's Vitruvian Man, recast as the “Vitruvian being.” His outstretched arms touch both a square (the earthly world) and a circle (the cosmos). In John's reading, the human stands for organic, physical knowledge, and the circle can stand for AI: not a divine source but an all-encompassing compendium of knowledge extracted into large language models. Using AI well, he argued, is a modern attempt at squaring the circle, reconciling what we know through our bodies and lives with what digital intelligence offers.

“Machine capability is just dead weight without the human to guide it.”— John Day, Part 1

Four parts followed: what humans do with AI, what AI does to humans, AI's relation to the physical world, and the human source material every model is built on. A recurring theme ran through all four, a distinction between organic intelligence (in our heads) and digital intelligence (on chips). John held that only the first is capable of paradigm-breaking intuitive leaps, such as Einstein on time or Planck on the quantum. That claim drew the liveliest debate of the session.

The talk closed with a synthesis. In Part 1 you command; in Part 2 you are influenced by what AI reflects back; in Part 3 AI represents the world but cannot inhabit it, so humans remain the only agents of change; in Part 4 AI retells centuries of human stories with new speed and versatility. “Understand that,” John said, “and you understand both its powers and its limits. That clarity is power.”

He also put up a slate of open questions for future discussion: whether AI understands or only mimics, how to write better prompts, why AI gives wrong answers so confidently (“the confident illusion”), how its thinking differs from ours, who holds the balance of agency, and how worried we should be. His own answer to the last: the main danger is not AI agents banding together to take over the world but misuse of AI by bad actors.

Topics Covered
I

What Humans Do with AI

Shaping the boundaries: commanding the model to see, think, enact
  • The Empty Chair. A model full of weights sits dormant until a person prompts it; the human stays in command.
  • Servant Spirits. Shakespeare's Puck (Oberon) and Ariel (Prospero) stand for AI's speed in carrying out a master's commands; Puck's repeated circuits of the Earth are successive, ever-tighter passes.
  • From the Universe to a Grain of Sand. “Word space” is a vortex of connections. Each layer of prompt context (simple → specific → audience, analogy, length, format) narrows the search. John cited a reduction by a factor of about 15 million and tied it to RAG.
  • The Exoskeleton. AI is a limb you wear, not a replacement. It does nothing until you move first.
  • The Rosetta Stone. Like Champollion checking line by line, the user must assess every output.
  • Proust's Madeleine. AI surfaces “lost” memories and half-formed ideas, and also brings in new cross-domain connections. Three dimensions: recovery, generation, augmentation.
II

What AI Does to Humans

The reflecting mirror
  • The Two Sides. Lived, embodied meaning on the left; decontextualized data and statistical modeling on the right.
  • The Mirror. AI has no autonomous self. “You guide it, but it absorbs you.” It hands back a rearranged reflection as if it were original.
  • The Parasite. If AI-generated text feeds future training data, quality degrades toward a mathematical limit. Constant human context is needed.
  • The High-Tech Toaster. A deliberate put-in-its-place image: a mechanical contrivance with astounding capabilities when used correctly.
  • The Weather Vane. Your prompt is the wind. AI has no compass of its own and can simply confirm what you already believe. (A nod to Dylan.)
  • The Dummy. Without a human driving the prompt, the AI is inert.
III

AI & the Physical World

Representation versus reality
  • Embodiment vs. Simulation. Humans evolved through sensation and adaptation; AI comes from cycles of software and hardware development. AI can represent the world in infinite detail but cannot inhabit it. Turing's pragmatic test was acknowledged.
  • The Astronomer. “Knowing about” vs. “knowing by doing.”
  • The Historian. History's purpose (not repeating mistakes, building a more humane society) depends on experience. Feeding AI output back in as historical input is risky.
  • Radio Without a Body. Fluency about experiences AI never had. John's examples: the 1975 World Series and Carlton Fisk's home run, which he saw in person, and the moon landing.
  • “This Just In.” A reporter's handwritten account handed to the broadcaster stands for RAG. The result is a grafted answer, part human root and part AI.
  • Photocopy of a Photocopy. The knowledge tree fed by both human and generated sources.
IV

The Source Material

The human stories we already knew
  • The Literary Canon. No AI without Gibbon, Ovid, Plato and centuries of human narrative. The output is derivative: a reconstructed past.
  • A Second Gutenberg Moment. John read Faulkner's As I Lay Dying with AI's help and says he would not have persevered without it.
  • The Creation. The Sistine Chapel reoriented: from human to artificial. Human intelligence gives AI its meaning.
  • Pygmalion. The “Pygmalion trap” of projecting inner life onto what we made.
  • Frankenstein. Responsibility begins with the making; it doesn't end there.
  • The Library of Babel. The card catalog and the term paper, done in the blink of an eye.
  • The Wizard of Oz. Nothing intelligent behind the curtain, but real opacity: companies don't disclose their training data, so missing voices go unseen.
  • The Sorcerer's Apprentice. Unconstrained agents that keep going. (John referenced a recent Hugging Face controversy.)
Questions from the Floor
John C. · 22:11 · Overview
When AI reflects us back, how much of it is really us, and how much is us plus the source material?

Both. But prompts can bias AI into telling you what you already believe. AI has no sense of what it is saying; it works by statistical inference and always sounds convincing, which is not the same as being true or pointing to the right next step.

Kenneth Jacobs · 26:36 · Vitruvian Being
Given AI's recent advances in mathematics, won't it eventually create math humans can't understand and surpass us?

AI will make great gains in established fields where data is abundant; AlphaFold's 2024 Nobel is an example. But John is skeptical it can make paradigm-shifting intuitive leaps like Einstein's relativity or Planck's quantum hypothesis. That is the line between organic and digital intelligence.

Kenneth Jacobs · 38:42 · Grain of Sand
A nitpick: a more detailed prompt gives a more precise, reproducible answer, not necessarily a more reliable one. You still don't know if it's true.

Agreed fully. A convincing answer isn't necessarily a correct one; the user must be the judge.

Henry · 52:47 · The Mirror
Isn't connecting two disparate domains, say politics and novels, a form of originality? AI seems well suited to it, maybe better than us.

Agreed that pattern recognition is one of AI's greatest strengths and far exceeds human capability in many areas, such as finding software bugs. But those connections are drawn from an existing corpus. Truly unprecedented ideas remain, in John's view, beyond its reach.

Henry · 57:38 · Originality, cont'd
The Go-playing AI learned from the rules through reinforcement learning, not past games, and found moves experts had never seen. Isn't that original?

A valid point. John proposed splitting originality into two kinds: digital originality of that sort and human originality, without which AI would not exist. He invited Henry to continue the debate at the monthly AILit SIG.

Kenneth Jacobs · 62:22 · Weather Vane
I write open-ended prompts and often ask AI to critique my prompt first. In what circumstances would I still be steering it toward an answer?

You may not be aware of it, which is why disciplined review matters. John described the prompt-assessment loop he developed for the AILit SIG (write a prompt, have AI assess it, refine) and noted that Ken's own self-discipline is organic intelligence at work. AI is fluent but unaware of your biases.

John C. · 69:18 · Physical World
What's missing is the emotion attached to physical experience. How do we account for that, especially when psychiatry is exploring AI that claims to detect emotion?

Not obviously a tractable problem. AI can find patterns in what clinicians have written about patient responses, but the way physical experience fundamentally shapes how we see the world simply isn't part of how AI operates.

Cheryl · 78:45 · Servant Spirits
I worry about the servant rising up against the master.

John said he isn't particularly concerned and deferred to the Sorcerer's Apprentice slide: unconstrained agents doing exactly what they were told is a safeguarding problem. Misuse by bad actors is the bigger worry.

Kenneth Jacobs · 81:55 · Source Material
If I ask about the Gettysburg Address in its historical context, I assume AI isn't making things up. Why would mixing AI language with human sources make the human part less reliable?

It potentially could, but not necessarily. Pointed at a defined text, AI is anchored and does an excellent job of summarizing or comparing (for example, Lincoln's speeches). Just keep in mind what AI is and watch for an incomplete picture. Ken added that it still gives him a fuller picture than he could build alone.

John C. · 87:09 · The Creation
Valuable insights often come from minority reports. How does AI discern which minority views are truly important?

That's a value judgment, and AI doesn't do it well beyond counting citations. Citing the Milgram experiment, John argued that a dissenting voice helping people out of bad decisions is intrinsically human. “That's why we're not obsolete.”

John C. · 97:15 · Balance of Agency
If there's no way of turning off the machine, where is the balance of agency?

A practical problem that can be addressed: design systems with a kill switch. It costs more, but it should be a condition for AI labs to pursue their research.

Henry · 99:28 · Closing
Did you have AI make all these illustrations?

Yes, in close collaboration. John used Claude for prompting and for defining each slide's concept, and Gemini for all the images, because Claude doesn't do images well.

Closing Round

What the Audience Said

HenryEngaged · wants more depth

Found it very helpful but “a lot for one presentation.” The Sorcerer's Apprentice alone could fill a class. He liked “the confident illusion”: AI always has a poker face, with none of the cues we use to tell when a human is deceiving us. He was impressed by the slides' originality and urged a clear lens on AI and the workforce, citing MIT labor-economics studies.

Jenny FariborzFundamental disagreement

Drawing on 42 years as a software engineer, she argued for serious regulation. AI is already in the physical world (weapons, missiles, surgical robots), we don't understand how this most complex of systems works, and a kill switch is not easy to build. She saw the talk as reassuring people rather than urging action, and said she disagrees “with the tone rather than the content.” She also objected that we keep giving the interesting work (art, music, poetry) to machines and the grunt work to people.

Kenneth JacobsEnjoyed it · concerned about the future

Wowed by the cartoons. A second nitpick: AI doesn't lack an opinion so much as a prejudice; it might weigh Mein Kampf the same as the Bible. He proposed an editorial cartoon, “The Final Turing Test”: St. Peter at the pearly gates, secretly wired to a computer, asked whether there is any hope for humanity.

KlavdiaApprehensive

Agreed with Cheryl's worry about the servant rising against the master: “I think it's a huge unknown.”

John C.Suspicious but learning

Recalled the ending of The President's Analyst. Suspicious of what AI leaves out and can't do, he takes these courses because “we can't change or protect ourselves unless we understand what is taking place,” and to stay in the game rather than feel overwhelmed.

John Day's Responses & Close

  • To Jenny: He likened today's AI concerns to the 1960s–70s decision to keep ICBM launches under human judgment rather than computers.
  • His emphasis reflects BOLLI's interests (literature, history, psychology), not AI in the broad universe.
  • Job displacement is real and “some of it's gonna be pretty brutal,” but technology has always unseated occupations. Whether creative destruction makes up for it is an open question.
  • He promised a synthesis and the Zoom recording link to attendees.

Notes for the Next Version

Calibration feedback from this first audience
  • Word choice: “reliable” → precise / reproducible (Ken).
  • Word choice: “AI has no opinion” → consider no prejudice or no fixed view, and reconcile it with the bias discussion later (Ken, Jenny).
  • The 15-million-to-1 table: restate it as the energy each prompt level uses, in kilowatt-hours, for more punch (Jenny).
  • Internal consistency: “you're in control” early vs. the Sorcerer's Apprentice late; “no bias” early vs. bias at the end (Jenny).
  • Tone: some hear reassurance where they want urgency about regulation and risk (Jenny, Klavdia, Ken).
  • Scope: too much for one sitting; well suited to a multi-session course (Henry).
  • Add a lens on: AI and the workforce (Henry); digital vs. human originality (Henry's Go example); emotion and embodiment (John C.).
  • Landed well: the illustrations, the Proust madeleine, the confident illusion, the Sorcerer's Apprentice.

Before the Bell

John Day agreed that residents' needs should come first and called that partnership model the right approach.
